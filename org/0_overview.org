- What is the limit of abstractions?
  #+begin_quote
  Abstractions are like a translation layer between two profoundly
  alien entities, one of whom (machines) are made by the other
  (man). They cannot turn machines into men or men into machines.

  Abstractions are also never loss-less: By sacrificing detail, and by
  creating a machine representation of reality, aspects of reality are
  suppressed.
  #+end_quote

* Truth: God is in the detail
#+attr_html: :width 300px:
[[../img/god_is_in_the_detail.png]]

- Paying attention to small details leads to excellence in computing.

- You're now getting immersed in a world of excruciating technical
  details:

  1. *Excruciating* because unlike in mathematics there isn't a theory
     or a clean, memorable hierarchy of axioms to hold on to.
     
  2. *Technical* (from the Greek word τεχνικός, "skillful" or "artful")
     because controlling machines depends on precision and tools that
     are learnt by practice rather than deduction.

  3. *Detailed* because every small piece matters: a missing semicolon,
     a wrong bracket, a single mistyped character can bring the whole
     structure down.

* Advice: How should you study?

- *Short list* from my [[https://github.com/birkenkrahe/org/blob/master/fall24/newFAQ.org#how-should-you-study-for-this-course][new FAQ (2024) on GitHub]]:

  1. Code every day no matter how little.

  2. Review lecture notes and notebooks.

  3. Participate actively in the class.

  4. Form study groups and/or join Lyon’s Programming Club.

  5. Complete assignments well before the deadline.

  6. Practice literate programming by documenting your process.

  7. Focus on fundamentals, simple examples, solid understanding.

  8. Drill yourself using the weekly quizzes, make small examples.

  9. Seek help when needed, on the chat or during office hours.

  10. Build a code & notebook repository at GitHub for your resume.

- *Extended advice:* On Google chat, I shared the 1994 Oxford U study
  guide for mathematics students, and I recommended you read
  pp. 1-10. Here is the summary:

  1. Your total working-time should be constent with full-time work.

  2. Attend all lectures and take notes (it does not matter how they
     look). Work through lecture material if you missed a lecture.

  3. If no lecture material is provided ask the instructor for it
     and/or ask for a meeting to go through the material briefly.

  4. Attempt to solve some problems related to the lecture. Use
     problems from the lecture first (with known answers).

  5. Use your own brain as a tutor to begin with. Challenge yourself
     with questions you don't immediately know the answer to.

  6. After a (self) tutorial session, give yourself an honest,
     rigorous check what you did not understand yet.

  7. Use books if you can. The books that have the greatest staying
     power are the ones that you connect with and that you like.

  8. Use the vacations and (weekend) breaks to consolidate what you
     have done during the preceding term (week), and to read ahead for
     the coming term (week).

  9. Create a GitHub account as a remote master copy of your
     programming work and use the profile to measure your activity.
     #+attr_html: :width 400px :float nil:
     [[../img/github.png]]

  10. Do more programming than feels necessary. Drill basic operations
      (I/O, string handling, memory allocation, problem analysis)
      until they become second nature. Join CSC 450 (see below).

* Advice: How you could study using AI

- For what it's worth: I fed this part to ChatGPT and asked about
  changes because of GAI - I mostly agree except *try not to use AI at
  all* because it is extremely hard for a *beginner* to know when he
  should stop trying. *Rule of thumb:* For any topic, allott twice the
  time, for any new topic, thrice the time you think you need.
  #+begin_quote
  1) *Lectures:* Attend all lectures as before—presence still
     matters. Use GAI to:
     - Clarify unclear definitions or steps after class.
     - Rephrase explanations in simpler terms or alternative
       perspectives.
     - Simulate follow-up Q&A on difficult parts of the lecture.
  2) *Private study*: Study actively, not passively—GAI is a tool, not a
     shortcut. Use GAI to:
     - Generate hints for exercises without revealing solutions.
     - Ask conceptual questions in your own words.
     - Test your understanding by explaining it to GAI and checking
       its paraphrase.
  3) *Problem-Solving:* Try every problem independently first. Use GAI
     when stuck to:
     - Request a single hint or next step.
     - Debug your reasoning or identify common mistakes.
     - Generate similar practice problems to reinforce concepts.
  4) *Tutorials:* Use GAI to simulate a mini-tutorial beforehand:
     - Practice explaining your solutions out loud to the AI.
     - Ask GAI to critique clarity or suggest alternate reasoning.
  5) *Vacation and Review Work*: Use GAI to:
     - Generate custom quizzes or flashcards.
     - Review old material interactively.
     - Preview future course content through exploratory questioning.
  6) *General Principle*
     - Always try first—then ask AI.
     - Use GAI to deepen understanding, not to shortcut thinking.
     - Combine traditional study skills with AI tools for best results.
  #+end_quote

* Objective: From CS consumer to CS producer

I found this in the preface to "An Infinite Descent into Pure
Mathematics" (free book at [[https://infinitedescent.xyz][infinitedescent.xyz]]) by Clive Newstead.

#+begin_quote
»Consumers of mathematics are expected to say how they used their
tools to find their answers. Producers of mathematics, on the other
hand, have to do much more: they must be able to keep track of
definitions and hypotheses, piece together facts in new and
interesting ways, and make their own definitions of mathematical
concepts. But even more importantly, once they have done this, they
must communicate their findings in a way that others find
intelligible, and they must convince others that what they have done
is correct, appropriate and worthwhile.«
#+end_quote

Adapted to computer science:

1) *Consumers of computer science* can say how they used their tools to
   find their answers to computational problems.

2) *Producers of computer science*:
   - Keep track of specifications and assumptions.
   - Combine algorithms and data structures in new & interesting ways.
   - Design their own abstract data types and systems.
   - Communicate their solutions in understandable ways.
   - Convince others that what they have built is correct, appropriate
     and worthwhile.
